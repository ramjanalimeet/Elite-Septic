Replacement Overview

When a Septic System Needs Replacing, Not Repairing

Septic systems do not last forever. A concrete tank may hold for forty years, but the drainfield in front of it is the part that wears out, and it wears out quietly. Soil that once absorbed effluent gradually clogs with the fine solids that escape a tank with a failed baffle or an overdue pumping schedule, until the ground simply stops taking water. At that point no amount of pumping fixes it, because the tank was never the problem.

Most replacement conversations around Lufkin start one of three ways. A drainfield has failed and the yard is wet. A tank has cracked, collapsed or corroded past repair. Or a household has outgrown a system that was sized for a much smaller house decades ago, and it now backs up every time there are guests.

Repair or replace: how to tell the difference

Repairs make sense when the failure is a component: a broken baffle, a cracked lid, a failed pump or float, a blocked line, a section of crushed pipe. Those are bounded problems with a known cost. Replacement becomes the honest answer when the drainfield itself has stopped absorbing, when the tank is no longer watertight, when the system is too small for the house it serves, or when repeated repairs have started stacking up toward the cost of doing it properly once.

We would rather tell you a system has years left in it than sell you a replacement you do not need. Where it is genuinely borderline we will say so, price both routes, and let you decide with the numbers in front of you.

What We Replace

Septic System Replacements We Handle

Not every replacement means starting from nothing. Sometimes the tank is sound and only the field needs rebuilding. These are the jobs we take on.

Complete System Replacement

Tank, lines, distribution and drainfield replaced as one job. This is the route when the whole system has aged out together, or when the original was never permitted or sized correctly for the property.

Septic Tank Replacement

A cracked, corroded, collapsed or no longer watertight tank swapped for a new one. Where the drainfield still has life in it, replacing the tank alone is the cheaper and entirely legitimate fix.

Drainfield and Leach Field Replacement

The most common replacement we do. Once soil has clogged, it does not recover, so a new field is built in fresh ground with trenches sized to what the soil will actually absorb.

Conventional Gravity Systems

Where the soil percolates well enough and the lot has fall to work with, a conventional tank and gravity trench system remains the simplest and least expensive thing to own and maintain.

Aerobic Treatment Units

Common across East Texas because of the clay. An aerobic unit treats effluent to a higher standard so it can be dispersed on ground that would never take a conventional field, and it comes with pumps, floats and a control panel.

Spray and Drip Dispersal Fields

Where surface or shallow dispersal is the workable answer, we install spray heads or subsurface drip lines with the chlorinator, pump tank and controls the system needs to run properly.

Pumped and Low-Pressure Systems

On lots where the field sits above the tank or the ground will not cooperate, effluent has to be pumped and dosed rather than allowed to run. That means a pump tank, floats, a panel and an alarm.

Undersized and Overloaded Systems

Added bedrooms, a shop bathroom, an RV hookup or simply more people than the original design allowed for. If the system is backing up under normal use, it is being asked to do more than it was built to.

Old, Failed and Abandoned Systems

Cesspools, undocumented tanks, homemade fields and systems installed before permitting caught up with them. Old tanks have to be properly pumped and either removed or filled, not simply left in the ground.

Step By Step

Our Septic System Replacement Process

A replacement is a permitted construction job, not a call-out. Here is how one runs from the first phone call to the final inspection.

1. Site Visit and Assessment

We come out, look at the existing system, and establish what has actually failed. Sometimes that changes the answer — a tank in good order in front of a dead field is a smaller job than a full replacement.

2. Soil and Site Evaluation

Soil borings or a backhoe pit tell us what the ground will absorb, where the water table sits, and how much usable area the lot has. On East Texas clay this is the step that decides whether you get a conventional field or an aerobic system.

3. System Design and Written Quote

The system is sized to the soil, the lot and the number of bedrooms, then drawn up. You get a flat-rate quote covering design, permit, materials, excavation and cleanup before anything is committed to.

4. Permitting

We file the application with the county authorized agent — in Angelina County that is the Angelina and Neches River Authority in Lufkin — and handle the back and forth. Permitting is usually the part that sets the schedule.

5. Old System Removed, New One Installed

The old tank is pumped and either removed or properly filled and abandoned, the new tank is set and levelled, and the new field or treatment unit goes in with lines, distribution and any pumps and controls it needs.

6. Inspection, Backfill and Cleanup

The system is inspected and signed off before it is covered, then backfilled, graded to shed water away from the field, and the site left tidy. You get the paperwork and a walk-through of how to look after it.

How long does a septic system replacement take?

The installation itself is usually two to four days on site for a conventional system, and a little longer for an aerobic unit with pumps, panels and spray or drip dispersal. The wider timeline is set by the soil evaluation and the permit rather than by the digging, so from first visit to finished system is more often two to six weeks. Wet weather extends it, because nobody should be excavating a drainfield in saturated ground. If you are working to a closing date or a build schedule, tell us at the first visit and we will work back from it.

Signs Your System Is Heading for Replacement

Replacement rarely arrives without warning. These are the signs that the system is running out of road:

  • Backups that return within weeks of every pump-out
  • Standing water or surfacing effluent over the drainfield
  • Ground over the field that stays soft when the rest of the yard is dry
  • A stripe of grass growing greener and faster over the trenches
  • Repairs stacking up, with a new fault every few months
  • A tank taking on groundwater or losing effluent through failed walls
  • A household that has grown past what the system was designed for

If two or three of those describe your property, an inspection is the cheapest next step. It will tell you whether you are looking at a repair, a new field, or a full replacement — before you spend money on the wrong one.

Cost and Planning

What Drives the Cost of a Septic System Replacement

There is no single price for a replacement, because two properties a mile apart can need very different systems. These are the things that move the number.

Soil type and percolation

This is the biggest single factor. Sandy loam that drains well supports a conventional gravity field, which is the least expensive system to build and to own. Heavy clay that will not percolate pushes you toward an aerobic treatment unit with spray or drip dispersal, and that is a materially bigger job.

System type and size

Systems are sized by the number of bedrooms and the expected daily flow, so a three-bedroom house and a five-bedroom house do not get the same field. Aerobic units add a pump tank, an air compressor, a control panel, an alarm and a chlorinator on top of the tank and dispersal area.

Site access and excavation

A flat, open lot with room for a truck and a backhoe is straightforward. Slopes, trees, rock, tight access, existing driveways and outbuildings all add time. So does having to route around a well, a pond or a property line at the required setback distance.

Removing the old system

An old tank cannot simply be left as it is. It has to be pumped out and then either removed from the ground or crushed and filled so it cannot collapse later. Old drainfield lines and any abandoned cesspool have to be dealt with properly at the same time.

Permits and design work

Every replacement needs a site evaluation, a design and a permit from the county authorized agent. We fold that into the quote rather than leaving you to chase it, and the permit timeline is usually what sets the start date rather than our schedule.

Restoration afterwards

Replacing a system means opening up a good part of a yard. Backfill, grading so surface water runs away from the new field, and tidying the work area are part of the job. Full landscaping and re-sodding is usually something owners prefer to arrange themselves.

Repair or Replace

Repair or Replace: How We Decide

A replacement is the largest bill a septic system will ever hand you, so it should never be the default answer. Here is how the two sides actually break down.

When a repair is the right call

  • A broken, missing or corroded inlet or outlet baffle
  • A cracked lid, damaged riser or blocked access port
  • A failed effluent pump, float switch, compressor or control panel
  • A blocked or root-damaged line between the house and the tank
  • A crushed or collapsed section of pipe that can be dug and replaced
  • A tank overdue for pumping, with a drainfield still absorbing normally
  • A single localised fault on an otherwise sound, correctly sized system

When replacement is the honest answer

  • A drainfield that has stopped absorbing and no longer recovers
  • Effluent surfacing or ponding over the field under normal use
  • A tank that is cracked, corroded or no longer watertight
  • Backups returning within weeks of every pump-out
  • A system undersized for the household now living in the house
  • Repairs that have started stacking toward the cost of replacement
  • A cesspool or unpermitted system with no records and no compliance

Warning Signs

Signs Your Septic System May Need Replacing

Any one of these on its own may only mean a repair. Several together usually mean the system has reached the end of its working life.

Backups that return after every pump-out

If the house drains normally for a fortnight after a pump and then backs up again, the problem sits downstream of the tank. That pattern points squarely at a drainfield that has stopped absorbing.

Effluent surfacing over the drainfield

Standing water or visible wastewater above the field means the soil is no longer taking what the system sends it. Once a field is surfacing under normal use, it does not recover on its own.

Ground that stays soft and spongy

Soil over the trenches that remains wet when the rest of the yard has dried out is saturated soil. It is the stage before surfacing, and it is the point at which you still have choices.

Bright green grass in stripes over the field

Grass growing greener, thicker and faster over the trenches is being fertilised by effluent sitting too close to the surface. It is the clearest sign visible from the yard and the most commonly ignored.

A tank that will not hold water

Cracked walls, corroded penetrations, a failed lid seat or a liquid level sitting wrong all point to a tank taking groundwater in or losing effluent out. Both are damaging, and past a point neither is patchable.

Repairs every few months

One fault is a repair. A new fault every quarter is a system telling you it has aged out. At some point the running total of repairs passes the cost of doing the job properly once.

A system too small for the household

Added bedrooms, a shop bathroom, an RV hookup or simply more people than the original design allowed. If it backs up under ordinary use, it is being asked for more than it was built to give.

Failure every time it rains

A field that only backs up after several days of rain has almost no capacity in reserve. Each wet spell takes a little more, and East Texas gets plenty of them.

No permit, no records, no idea

Homemade systems, cesspools and undocumented tanks installed before permitting caught up with them. If you cannot establish what is in the ground or whether it was ever legal, replacement is often the only clean answer.

Not sure which of these you are looking at? A septic inspection will tell you whether this is a repair, a new drainfield or a full replacement — before you spend anything on the wrong fix.

Septic System Replacement FAQs

Common questions about replacing a septic system in Lufkin and across East Texas. If yours is not here, call and ask.

How do I know if my septic system needs replacing?

The clearest indicator is a backup that returns within weeks of every pump-out. That pattern means the problem sits downstream of the tank, in a drainfield that has stopped absorbing. Other signs are effluent surfacing over the field, ground that stays soft when the rest of the yard is dry, a stripe of noticeably greener grass over the trenches, a tank that is cracked or no longer watertight, and repairs arriving every few months. An inspection is the only way to be certain, because it measures sludge and scum levels, checks the tank and baffles, and walks the field rather than guessing from the symptoms.

How much does a septic system replacement cost in Texas?

There is no single figure, because two properties a mile apart can need very different systems. The main drivers are soil type, system type and size, and site access. Sandy loam that percolates well supports a conventional gravity field, which is the least expensive option. Heavy clay usually pushes you toward an aerobic treatment unit with a pump tank, compressor, control panel and spray or drip dispersal, which is a materially larger job. Bedroom count sets the sizing, and slopes, trees, rock or tight access add excavation time. We give free estimates and a flat rate covering design, permit, materials, excavation and cleanup before any work starts.

How long does it take to replace a septic system?

The installation is typically two to four days on site for a conventional system, and longer for an aerobic unit with pumps, panels and dispersal components. The overall timeline is set by the soil evaluation and the permit rather than the digging, so from first visit to a finished, inspected system is more often two to six weeks. Prolonged wet weather extends it, because excavating a drainfield in saturated ground damages the soil structure you are relying on. If you are working to a closing date or a build schedule, say so at the first visit.

Do I need a permit to replace a septic system?

Yes. In Texas, replacing an on-site sewage facility requires a permit from the local authorized agent, along with a site and soil evaluation and a system design. In Angelina County that is the Angelina and Neches River Authority in Lufkin, and the neighbouring counties each work through their own agent. We handle the evaluation, the design and the permit application as part of the job, and the system is inspected and signed off before it is covered.

Can I replace only the drainfield instead of the whole system?

Often, yes, and it is the most common replacement we do. The tank and the drainfield age at different rates: a concrete tank can remain sound for decades while the field in front of it clogs and fails. If an inspection shows the tank is watertight, structurally sound and correctly sized, a new drainfield alone is a legitimate and considerably cheaper fix. It still needs a soil evaluation, a design and a permit, because the new field has to be built in ground that will actually absorb.

What happens to the old septic tank?

It cannot simply be left as it is. The old tank is pumped out and then either removed from the ground entirely or crushed and filled so it cannot collapse into a void later, which is a genuine safety issue on a property with children or livestock. Old drainfield lines and any abandoned cesspool are dealt with at the same time. Proper decommissioning is part of the permitted work, not an optional extra.

Conventional or aerobic: which system will I need?

The soil decides, not preference. A conventional tank and gravity trench system needs soil that percolates well enough to absorb effluent and enough usable area at the required setbacks. Much of the clay around Lufkin will not do that, which is why aerobic treatment units are so common here. An aerobic unit treats effluent to a higher standard so it can be dispersed by spray heads or drip lines on ground a conventional field could never use. It costs more to install and it needs ongoing maintenance under a contract, but on clay it is frequently the only workable design.

What maintenance does a new system need?

A conventional system wants pumping every three to five years depending on household size and tank capacity, and the field kept clear of vehicles, structures and deep-rooted trees. An aerobic system is a different commitment: Texas requires it to be kept under an active maintenance contract with regular inspections by a licensed maintenance provider, and it has a pump, floats, an air compressor, a chlorinator and an alarm that all need checking. We walk you through the routine for whichever system you end up with before we leave.

Will replacing my septic system tear up the yard?

Some of it, unavoidably. A replacement means excavating for the tank and opening trenches for the new field, so a good part of a yard is disturbed. We keep the working area as contained as access allows, backfill, and grade so surface water runs away from the new field rather than sitting on it. Cleanup and grading are included; full landscaping and re-sodding is usually something owners prefer to arrange themselves once the ground has settled.
