Septic Permits in Angelina County, TX: A Step-by-Step Guide

If you are building a home, replacing a failed system or buying rural property around Lufkin, a septic permit is one of the first things to sort out. It is also one of the most misunderstood. Many people assume the county courthouse handles it, that a permit is a quick form, or that an old system carries over automatically when a property changes hands. None of those is quite right.

This guide explains exactly how septic permitting works in Angelina County: which agency issues permits, when you need one, each step from the site evaluation to the License to Operate, the extra requirements for aerobic systems, the setback rules that shape where a system can go, and what happens when a property with a septic system is sold.

Who issues septic permits in Angelina County?

In Texas, septic systems are officially called on-site sewage facilities, or OSSFs. The state rules are set by the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) in 30 TAC Chapter 285. TCEQ delegates day-to-day permitting to local “authorized agents” wherever a local entity has an approved program.

In Angelina County, that authorized agent is the Angelina & Neches River Authority (ANRA), not the county itself. ANRA also regulates septic systems in San Augustine County and within Control Zone Rayburn, the regulated zone around Sam Rayburn Reservoir. Its OSSF department is based in Lufkin and handles new permits, inspections, license transfers and septic complaints. Local orders can be stricter than the state minimum, so ANRA’s rules are the ones that apply to your property.

When do you need a septic permit?

Under ANRA’s program, if public sewer service is available, a property must connect to it. If it is not, the owner needs a permit to construct, alter, repair, extend or operate a septic system. In practice that covers:

  • A new system for a new home, manufactured home, shop or cabin.
  • Replacing a failed system or drain field.
  • Adding bedrooms or living space that increases the design flow beyond what the existing system was permitted for.
  • Converting a conventional system to an aerobic system, or making other significant changes.
  • Transferring an existing licensed system to a new owner.

There are limited exceptions in state law, including for some older systems installed before local programs began and for certain single-family homes on large tracts. They come with conditions, so check with ANRA before assuming any exemption applies to you.

The permit process, step by step

ANRA describes a standard path from bare ground to a licensed, operating system. Here is how it works in practice.

Step 1: Site evaluation by a licensed site evaluator

A TCEQ-licensed site evaluator examines the soil where the system will go, usually by digging test pits or borings below the proposed trench depth. They classify the soil, look for signs of seasonal groundwater, identify any restrictive layers such as dense clay or rock, and note slopes, drainage and flood risk. The result decides whether the site can take a conventional system or needs an aerobic system or another alternative.

Step 2: System design

Using the site evaluation, a registered sanitarian or professional engineer drafts the system design, called planning materials. It sizes the tank from the home’s bedroom count and square footage, sizes the drain field or spray area from the soil class, and shows the layout with all required setbacks. Our septic tank sizing guide explains how those numbers are calculated.

Step 3: Aerobic affidavits, if required

If the site needs an aerobic system, ANRA requires two notarized affidavits to the public. One is recorded at the County Clerk’s office (with the volume and page number shown on the front), and the other is filed with ANRA along with the application. The recorded affidavit puts future buyers on notice that the property has an aerobic system with ongoing maintenance obligations.

Step 4: Application and fee

The application packet goes to ANRA with the Permit to Construct application form, the fee from ANRA’s current fee schedule, and the originals of the site evaluation, the design, any affidavit and the aerobic system information sheet. Incomplete packets are the most common cause of delay.

Step 5: Permit to Construct

ANRA reviews the packet and, once it is approved, issues the Permit to Construct. According to ANRA, it can usually do this within days, although state law allows an authorized agent up to 30 days. The permit is valid for one year from the date it is issued.

Step 6: Installation by a licensed installer

With the permit in hand, the owner hires a TCEQ-licensed installer to build the system exactly as designed. Standard systems can be installed by an Installer I or II; aerobic and other non-standard systems require an Installer II. The installer must request a construction inspection from ANRA at least five working days before the system will be ready.

Step 7: Inspection and License to Operate

An ANRA inspector checks the installation against the approved design before it is covered. Once it passes, ANRA issues a License to Operate to the property owner and keeps a copy on file. One important deadline: if the installer does not request the construction inspection within one year of the permit being issued, the authorization expires and the owner has to reapply and pay the fee again.

Extra requirements for aerobic systems

Aerobic treatment units are common in East Texas because so much of the soil is clay that drains too slowly for a conventional trench. They come with ongoing obligations. According to ANRA:

  • The state requires an active service contract with a licensed professional for the life of an aerobic system, plus annual testing, unless the owner qualifies for the single-family residential exemption.
  • Even with that exemption, a service contract is required for the first two years, with an inspection every four months.
  • After the first two years, an eligible owner can choose to maintain the system personally, but must carry out the inspections and send reports to ANRA.
  • A system that is not maintained to state standards can have its License to Operate revoked, and owners can face fines.

ANRA strongly recommends keeping a maintenance contract for the life of the system, and so do we. Aerobic systems have pumps, aerators, alarms and chlorinators that need regular attention. See our septic maintenance service for what each visit covers.

Setback distances that shape where a system can go

Texas rules set minimum distances between septic components and wells, water, buildings and property lines. These often decide where a system fits on a small or sloping lot, and on lake lots they can decide what kind of system is possible at all. A few of the key state minimums:

Private water well50 ft100 ft
Public water well50 ft150 ft

Spray and drip systems have their own distances, and ANRA’s local order or Control Zone Rayburn rules can be stricter than these state figures. Your designer will show every setback on the plan.

Buying or selling a property with a septic system

Under Texas law a septic system is part of the real property, and the license follows the deed. But the transfer is not automatic. ANRA requires a formal license transfer: the new owner submits a transfer application and a transfer fee (currently $35 according to ANRA). An unlicensed system cannot be transferred, which is one more reason to check the permit file before closing.

If the property has an aerobic system, ANRA recommends having the original manufacturer or dealer inspect all components and update the service policy, with a copy of the report sent to ANRA, and keeping the system under a maintenance policy. For buyers, we also recommend an independent inspection before closing. Our guide to septic inspections before buying a home in Lufkin explains what to ask for.

What to have ready before you start

The permit itself moves quickly once ANRA has a complete packet. Most delays happen before that, while information is being gathered. Having these ready makes every step faster:

  • The property’s legal description and a survey or plat, showing boundaries, easements and any existing structures.
  • House plans or at least the bedroom count and heated square footage, since both set the design flow and tank size.
  • The location of any water well on the property or on neighboring lots near the line, because well setbacks can decide where the system goes.
  • Plans for other improvements such as a pool, shop, barn or driveway, so they are not placed on top of the future drain field.
  • Existing permit records if you are replacing or altering an old system. ANRA can tell you what is on file.
  • A decision on the house site. Moving the house after the design is done usually means redesigning the septic system too.

Repairs and emergencies

Not every septic job starts with a new build. When an existing system fails, the same rules apply to the fix. Replacing a drain field, replacing a tank or making significant changes to a system needs a permit, a design and an inspection, just like a new system. Minor repairs and routine maintenance, such as replacing a pump, a baffle or a lid, or pumping the tank, generally do not.

If sewage is backing up into the house or surfacing in the yard, deal with the immediate hazard first: stop using water, keep people and pets away, and call a licensed professional. A good contractor will stabilize the situation and then explain which part of the permanent repair needs ANRA’s involvement. Our 24/7 emergency septic service covers Angelina County and the surrounding counties.

Common mistakes that cause delays

  • Buying land before checking the soil. A lot that needs an aerobic system costs more to develop. Get a site evaluation before you close on raw land if you can.
  • Planning the house first and the septic system last. Setbacks and drain field area can limit where the house, driveway and shop can go.
  • Incomplete applications. Missing originals, unsigned forms or a missing affidavit send the packet back.
  • Letting the permit expire. Installation and inspection must happen within a year of issue.
  • Hiring an unlicensed installer. Unpermitted or unlicensed work can fail inspection and has to be redone.
  • Forgetting the transfer when buying. Complete the license transfer promptly after closing.

Outside Angelina County

Neighboring counties have their own arrangements, and the rules and fees differ. Properties on the Sam Rayburn shoreline fall under Control Zone Rayburn wherever they are, and other counties we work in, such as Jasper, Polk, Trinity, Houston, Rusk and Sabine, each have their own authorized agent or are handled through the TCEQ regional office. We confirm the permitting authority for every job before any design work starts. See our service areas for the counties we cover.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get a septic permit in Angelina County?

Once ANRA has a complete application, the Permit to Construct is usually issued within days, and state law allows up to 30 days. The site evaluation and design come first, so plan for a few weeks from start to permit in a typical case.

Do I need a permit to repair my septic system?

Replacing a drain field, replacing a tank or significantly altering a system requires a permit. Routine maintenance such as pumping, cleaning a filter or replacing a pump does not. If you are unsure, ask before starting work.

How much does a septic permit cost?

ANRA publishes its current fees on its OSSF overview page. Remember that the permit fee is only one part of the cost; the site evaluation, design and installation are separate. Our guide to septic system costs in East Texas covers the full budget.

What happens if my system was never permitted?

An unlicensed system cannot be transferred when the property is sold, and if it fails or causes a nuisance, it will need to be brought into compliance. Talk to ANRA or a licensed professional about the options for your property.
